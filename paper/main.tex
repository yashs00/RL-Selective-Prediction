\begin{abstract}
Selective classifiers abstain on inputs they are unlikely to classify
correctly, trading coverage for reliability. The dominant heuristic --
thresholding the base model's maximum softmax probability (Chow's rule) --
is Bayes-optimal only when the model's posteriors are correct, an
assumption broken by miscalibration, distribution shift, and epistemic
uncertainty. We ask whether aggregating a heterogeneous bank of
uncertainty signals (calibrated confidence, ensemble disagreement, an
energy/OOD score, and feature-space geometry) improves the
accuracy--coverage trade-off over the best \emph{single} signal.

Our first result is negative and structural. On a binary task, every
logit-derived (Tier-A) signal we consider -- maximum softmax probability,
predictive entropy, both top-1-minus-top-2 margins, temperature-scaled
confidence, and a correctly-centred energy score -- is a strictly monotone
transform of every other. We prove this and confirm it empirically at
$|\rho_{\mathrm{Spearman}}| = 1.0000$ on three binary datasets. Because
the risk--coverage curve depends only on the induced ranking, an entire
family of ostensibly distinct confidence measures collapses to a single
ordering, and no aggregator over them can differ from MSP however it is
trained. This offers a structural explanation for a run of negative
results in the recent selective-classification literature.

Where the signal bank is genuinely non-degenerate, aggregation does help,
but modestly and inconsistently: across six datasets and 10 seeds, a
simple stacked aggregator beats the best single signal by
$6.2$--$6.4\%$ AURC on three datasets (bootstrap CIs excluding zero) while
\emph{harming} it on two. We report three further negative findings that
bear on how such systems are built: ensemble-disagreement signals never
helped; the simplest aggregator (logistic-regression stacking)
outperformed neural aggregators trained with coverage-targeted losses; and
selective abstention widened, never narrowed, subgroup risk disparities.
We additionally show that a Hoeffding--Bentkus risk-control bound makes
distribution-free selective-risk guarantees practically attainable where a
plain Hoeffding bound abstains entirely. We are explicit about what we
cannot explain: no property we measured -- class count, or the quality of
the geometric signals -- predicts which datasets benefit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chow's rule~\cite{chow1970} -- abstain when the maximum predicted
posterior falls below a threshold -- is the Bayes-optimal selective
classifier \emph{if} the model's posteriors are the true class posteriors.
They rarely are: neural and tree-based classifiers are commonly
miscalibrated~\cite{guo2017calibration}, behave unpredictably on
out-of-distribution inputs~\cite{liu2020energy}, and carry epistemic
uncertainty that a single forward pass cannot express. A large benchmark
of selective-classification methods across dozens of datasets found that
no single method dominates across operating points and
objectives~\cite{pugnana2024benchmark}, which is the central motivation for an
\emph{adaptive}, multi-signal approach rather than one more single-signal
heuristic.

At the same time, a well-tuned softmax-confidence baseline is a
notoriously strong competitor: several recent studies show that elaborate
learned-abstention methods fail to beat it
in-distribution~\cite{feng2023better}, and that cheap post-hoc fixes (
temperature scaling, logit normalization) recover much of the claimed gain
of heavier methods~\cite{cattelan2023logitnorm}. We treat this as a
falsifiable prediction rather than a threat to route around: we
pre-register (\S\ref{sec:rq}) that any advantage from aggregation is
\emph{regime-dependent} -- small or absent in-distribution, and larger
under distribution shift and at low coverage, where a single signal's
blind spots matter most.

\textbf{Contributions.} We state these as they turned out, including where
the outcome contradicted our own pre-registered expectation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A proof, with empirical confirmation, that the
    logit-derived signal family is rank-degenerate on binary tasks}
    ($|\rho| = 1.0000$ across three datasets). This bounds what
    \emph{any} aggregator over such a bank can achieve, and explains a
    run of negative results in the literature without appealing to
    implementation error.
  \item \textbf{A conditional, quantified answer on aggregation}: it beats
    the best single signal by $6.2$--$6.4\%$ AURC on three of six
    datasets and harms it on two, with bootstrap intervals excluding zero
    in all five cases. We were unable to determine what distinguishes the
    two groups, and say so rather than supply a post-hoc mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Three negative engineering findings} that we believe
    generalise: ensemble-disagreement signals never helped; a
    parameter-free or linear aggregator beat the neural,
    coverage-targeted aggregator we designed; and coverage-targeted
    losses are unstable unless the operating threshold is derived as an
    empirical quantile rather than learned (an $8\times$ reduction in
    seed variance).
  \item \textbf{Practical distribution-free risk control}, showing that
    the choice of concentration bound decides whether the guarantee is
    usable at all, and an audit finding that abstention \emph{widens}
    subgroup risk disparities.
\end{enumerate}

We pre-registered (\S\ref{sec:rq}) that any advantage would be
regime-dependent. That prediction was not confirmed: the pattern we found
does not track distribution shift, and we report the pre-registered
expectation alongside its refutation rather than silently reframing it.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rq}
\textbf{RQ1.} Does aggregating heterogeneous uncertainty signals improve
the accuracy-coverage trade-off over the best single-signal abstention
rule?
\textbf{RQ2.} Is that advantage regime-dependent -- roughly tied with
confidence thresholding in-distribution, clearly better under shift and at
low coverage?
\textbf{RQ3.} Can the learned score be wrapped in a finite-sample
guarantee on selective risk without losing coverage relative to a
heuristic threshold?
\textbf{RQ4.} Does multi-criteria abstention reduce the concentration of
abstentions (and residual error) on minority subgroups documented by prior
work~\cite{jones2021disparities}?

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Foundations.} Chow~\cite{chow1970} established the optimal reject
rule: abstain when the maximum posterior falls below a threshold, which is
Bayes-optimal \emph{given correct posteriors}. El-Yaniv and
Wiener~\cite{elyaniv2010foundations} formalised the risk--coverage
trade-off, and Geifman and El-Yaniv~\cite{geifman2017selective} established
the evaluation protocol we follow. Our binary-degeneracy result
(\S\ref{sec:degeneracy}) is a statement about this same setting: it
identifies a regime in which Chow's rule is not merely a strong baseline
but an \emph{unimprovable} one for any method restricted to logit-derived
signals.

\textbf{Learned abstention.} SelectiveNet~\cite{geifman2019selectivenet}
adds a selection head with a coverage constraint; Deep
Gamblers~\cite{liu2019deepgamblers} recasts abstention as an extra class;
ConfidNet~\cite{corbiere2019confidnet} learns the true-class probability
with an auxiliary head; Self-Adaptive Training~\cite{huang2020sat} exploits
training dynamics; CRL~\cite{moon2020crl} regularises confidence ranking
directly; and learning-to-defer~\cite{mozannar2020defer} targets the
human-in-the-loop variant. All require retraining or modifying the base
model. Our framework is strictly post-hoc, which is what allows the same
signal bank and aggregator to wrap a frozen model of any type -- but our
results also show that this flexibility buys less than we expected.

\textbf{The strong-baseline literature.} Feng et
al.~\cite{feng2023better} argue that a properly tuned softmax baseline
matches elaborate learned-abstention methods, and Cattelan and
Silva~\cite{cattelan2023logitnorm} show that cheap post-hoc repairs
recover much of the claimed gain. Our findings agree with theirs and
supply a mechanism for part of the phenomenon: on binary tasks the
competing signals are not merely similar in performance, they are
\emph{provably rank-identical}, so no amount of method sophistication can
separate them. We also note a limitation of the logit-normalisation
repair specifically: it is degenerate at $K{=}2$, where dividing by the
logit norm removes precisely the magnitude that carries the confidence
information, leaving only the predicted class. This appears to be a
property of the published method rather than of any implementation.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} Pugnana et al.~\cite{pugnana2024benchmark} benchmark
18 selective-classification methods over 44 datasets and find that no
method dominates across operating points -- the observation that motivated
this work's adaptive framing. Our contribution is complementary in kind:
they establish \emph{that} no method dominates; we give a proof of one
structural reason why a large class of them cannot.

\textbf{Signal sources.} Our bank draws on temperature
scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration}, deep
ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} and
MC-dropout~\cite{gal2016dropout} for epistemic uncertainty, the energy
score~\cite{liu2020energy}, Mahalanobis distance~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis},
$k$-NN distance~\cite{sun2022knn}, trust score~\cite{jiang2018trustscore}
and GradNorm~\cite{huang2021gradnorm} for out-of-distribution and
geometric evidence. A finding worth flagging for practitioners is that the
OOD-derived signals, developed largely in vision, transferred poorly to
tabular data: $k$-NN distance and Mahalanobis were \emph{worse than random
abstention} on several datasets, while trust score was the single best
signal on one.

\textbf{Fairness.} Jones et al.~\cite{jones2021disparities} show that
selective classification can magnify accuracy disparities across groups.
We reproduce this failure mode rather than fix it (\S\ref{sec:results}):
multi-signal abstention widened subgroup gaps and never narrowed them.

\textbf{Risk control.} We wrap the abstention score using conformal risk
control~\cite{vovk2005conformal, angelopoulos2022riskcontrol,
angelopoulos2021learnthentest}; see~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle} for an
accessible treatment. Our contribution here is empirical rather than
theoretical: the choice of concentration inequality determines whether the
resulting guarantee is usable at practical risk levels at all.

\textbf{The gap.} Across this literature, learned-abstention methods
require base-model retraining; none ships a distribution-free guarantee;
and evaluation under shift and across subgroups is inconsistent. This work
occupies the remaining cell -- post-hoc, guaranteed, and audited for
disparity -- while reporting candidly that occupying it yields a
conditional and modest benefit rather than a uniform one.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}
Let $f$ be a fixed, frozen $K$-class base classifier and $g: \mathcal{X}
\to \{0,1\}$ a selection function; $(f,g)$ abstains on $x$ when $g(x)=0$.
\textbf{Coverage} is $\Pr[g(x)=1]$. \textbf{Selective risk} is the error
rate of $f$ restricted to the accepted region,
$R(f,g) = \mathbb{E}[\ell(f(x),y) \cdot g(x)] / \mathbb{E}[g(x)]$.
The \textbf{risk-coverage curve} plots selective risk as a function of the
target coverage achieved by thresholding a score $s(x)$; \textbf{AURC} is
its area, and \textbf{E-AURC} subtracts the AURC of the best achievable
(oracle) ordering, which controls for the confounding effect of base
accuracy when aggregating across datasets~\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Signal bank}
Given $x$, we compute $u(x) \in \mathbb{R}^m$ from four tiers: (A)
logit-derived signals available from a single forward pass -- max softmax
probability, entropy, margin, negative energy, logit-norm-normalized MSP,
temperature-scaled MSP; (B) model-internal signals requiring multiple
forward passes or an ensemble -- vote entropy, mean pairwise KL, top-class
variance; (C) representation/data-geometry signals requiring the training
set at inference -- $k$NN distance, local label agreement, trust
score~\cite{jiang2018trustscore}, Mahalanobis distance to the predicted
class centroid~\cite{lee2018mahalanobis}. Tree-specific signals
(out-of-bag or boosting-round disagreement, leaf co-occurrence distance)
are a natural extension for GBM base models but are not implemented here.

\subsection{Aggregator}
We compare three aggregator classes of increasing expressiveness: A0, a
learning-free rank/z-score average; A1, logistic regression or LightGBM
stacked on $u(x)$ to predict $\mathbf{1}[f(x)\neq y]$ (ordinary stacking);
and A2, the same function class as A1 trained instead with a
coverage-targeted loss (\S\ref{sec:loss}).

\subsection{Coverage-targeted loss}
\label{sec:loss}
Let $g(x) = \sigma((\tau - s(x))/T)$ be a soft acceptance gate. We
minimize
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_\kappa = \frac{\sum_i g(x_i)\,\ell(f(x_i),y_i)}{\sum_i g(x_i)}
  + \lambda \max(0, \kappa - \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_i g(x_i))^2,
\end{equation}
with $\tau$ a learned threshold, which trains the aggregator to rank well
\emph{at the coverage the system is actually deployed at} rather than for
correctness-classification accuracy overall. We also report an AURC
surrogate (summing $\mathcal{L}_\kappa$ over a coverage grid) and a
pairwise ranking loss (logistic loss on $s(\text{incorrect}) -
s(\text{correct})$ margins).

\subsection{Instance-adaptive signal weighting}
A gating network $w(x) = \mathrm{softmax}(h(u(x)))$ produces
per-instance weights over the (standardized) signals, $s(x) =
\sum_j w_j(x)\, \tilde u_j(x)$, so the model can route to different
signals for different failure regimes (e.g. Mahalanobis distance for
far-OOD inputs, local label agreement for ambiguous in-distribution ones).

\subsection{Conformal risk-control wrapper}
On a held-out calibration set of size $n$, we select the largest threshold
$\hat\tau$ such that a finite-sample upper confidence bound on selective
risk stays below a user target $\alpha$, giving
$\Pr[R(f, g_{\hat\tau}) \le \alpha] \ge 1-\delta$ under exchangeability,
independent of how the aggregator was trained. This guarantee is valid
in-distribution; under distribution shift, exchangeability fails and the
guarantee is evaluated empirically rather than claimed.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Datasets.} Six OpenML datasets: three binary (Adult, German
Credit, and Electricity, the last split temporally to induce distribution
shift) and three multiclass (Satimage $K{=}6$, Wine Quality $K{=}7$,
Letter $K{=}26$). All are drawn from curated suites (OpenML-CC18 /
Grinsztajn et al.) rather than hand-assembled, so dataset choice cannot be
attacked as cherry-picked.

Candidate multiclass datasets were screened on \emph{test-set error
count} before admission, which is the binding constraint for selective
prediction and is easy to overlook: the risk--coverage curve is built
entirely from the errors in $D_{\text{test}}$, so an easy dataset yields a
curve made of noise regardless of its row count. This screen rejected four
otherwise-attractive candidates that produced fewer than 12 test errors
apiece (Segment: 5; Pendigits: 11; Optdigits: 8; Texture: 9).

\textbf{Protocol.} A disjoint four-way split
($D_{\text{train}}$ 60\%, $D_{\text{meta}}$ 15\%, $D_{\text{cal}}$ 10\%,
$D_{\text{test}}$ 15\%); temporal datasets are split by row order, never
shuffled. Signals for aggregator training are computed by 5-fold
cross-fitting over $D_{\text{train}} \cup D_{\text{meta}}$, so no signal
vector is ever produced by a model that saw the row it describes
(\S\ref{sec:method}); we verify this with a memorising-model test.
Temperature scaling and the calibration residual are fit on
$D_{\text{meta}}$ using a model trained only on $D_{\text{train}}$. The
base model is LightGBM (300 trees) with row and column subsampling enabled
so that the random seed genuinely varies the fitted model. Every
configuration is run at 10 seeds.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} We report AURC (area under the
risk--coverage curve; lower is better), with E-AURC, risk at fixed
coverage, and failure-prediction AUROC in the supplementary results file.
Significance uses two complementary tests: a per-seed paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank test with Holm--Bonferroni correction across methods, and a
paired bootstrap over test instances (the same resampled indices applied
to both methods). For the bootstrap we report both a pooled interval and
an interval for the \emph{mean} effect across seeds, since the two answer
different questions; claims in this paper are based on the latter, and
both are released.

\textbf{A note on the comparison baseline.} We compare aggregators against
the best single signal on each dataset, not only against MSP. As
Table~\ref{tab:agg_vs_single} shows, these differ sharply on one dataset,
and reporting only the MSP comparison would materially overstate what
aggregation contributes.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Tier-A signals are rank-degenerate on binary tasks}
\label{sec:degeneracy}

A softmax reads only logit \emph{differences}, so at $K{=}2$ the entire
Tier-A family is a function of the single scalar margin $s$. MSP,
entropy, both margins, temperature-scaled MSP and centred energy are
therefore strictly monotone transforms of one another, and since AURC
depends only on the induced ordering, all six produce \emph{identical}
risk--coverage curves. Measured $|\rho_{\mathrm{Spearman}}|$ against MSP
is $1.0000$ for all five comparators on all three binary datasets. The
consequence is immediate and, we think, under-appreciated: on a binary
task, a signal bank of six confidence measures contains exactly
\emph{one} ranking, and no aggregator over it can improve on MSP.

At $K \ge 3$ the degeneracy breaks, but only partially and unevenly.
Energy decorrelates most ($1.0000 \to 0.9608$ on Satimage, $0.7089$ on
Wine), whereas entropy remains at $1.0000$ on Satimage. Multiclass
structure is therefore \emph{sufficient} to break exact degeneracy but
does not by itself guarantee a diverse bank.

\subsection{Aggregation: modest, conditional, and not universally positive}

Table~\ref{tab:main_aurc} gives the headline AURCs and
Table~\ref{tab:agg_vs_single} the comparison that actually tests
aggregation. Three datasets show a consistent benefit over the best single
signal -- $-6.2\%$ (German Credit), $-6.4\%$ (Satimage), $-6.3\%$ (Letter)
-- a striking agreement across $K{=}2$, $6$ and $26$ and across AURCs
spanning three orders of magnitude. Seed-averaged bootstrap intervals
exclude zero in all three cases. On two datasets aggregation \emph{hurts}
(Adult $+0.9\%$, Electricity $+0.4\%$), also with intervals excluding
zero. These are not ambiguous outcomes: aggregation reliably helps on
some datasets and reliably harms on others.

Wine Quality deserves separate comment because it is the case most likely
to be misreported. Against MSP the best aggregator improves AURC by
$24.5\%$, the largest effect we observe, and ten methods clear
Holm correction. But the best \emph{single} signal on that dataset is not
MSP: plain trust score attains $0.1412$ against MSP's $0.1846$, and the
aggregator adds only a further $1.3\%$ on top of simply using it. The
honest statement is that MSP is the wrong signal for this dataset, not
that aggregation is powerful here. We consider this practically the more
useful finding of the two.

\subsection{RQ3: distribution-free risk control}

Table~\ref{tab:conformal} reports coverage attained at a guaranteed
selective risk $\le \alpha$. Replacing the standard Hoeffding bound with
the Hoeffding--Bentkus bound is what makes this table non-trivial: on
20{,}000 well-behaved synthetic calibration points the Hoeffding bound
admits \emph{zero} coverage at $\alpha \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05\}$, while
Hoeffding--Bentkus recovers $0.117$ and $0.417$ at $\alpha = 0.02$ and
$0.05$. Zeros that remain in Table~\ref{tab:conformal} are correct
behaviour rather than failure: a risk target below the base model's own
error rate cannot be met at any positive coverage.

\subsection{RQ4: subgroup disparity}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroup} reports the max--min subgroup selective-risk gap.
Aggregation does not reduce disparity on any dataset we measured. On
Adult, eight methods significantly \emph{widen} the gap relative to MSP
(Holm-corrected $p<0.05$) and none narrow it, reproducing the disparity
amplification of Jones et al.~\cite{jones2021disparities} rather than
mitigating it. Practitioners adopting multi-signal abstention for
reliability reasons should not assume it is fairness-neutral.

\section{Ablations and analysis}
\label{sec:ablations}

\textbf{Ensemble disagreement (Tier B) never helped.} Adding a five-member
ensemble's vote entropy, mean pairwise KL and top-class variance did not
improve any dataset, and diluted the aggregator's advantage where one
existed (on Wine, $-1.3\% \to -0.2\%$ against the best single signal). The
Tier-B signals are individually weak -- on Wine, vote entropy scores
$0.292$ against random abstention's $0.320$. Genuinely independent signals
are not automatically \emph{informative} signals, and each additional
column costs the aggregator another parameter to fit from a small
meta-set.

\textbf{The simplest aggregator wins.} Logistic-regression stacking is the
best aggregator on Satimage, Wine and Letter, and unsupervised rank
averaging -- which has no learned parameters at all -- is best on German
Credit. The neural aggregators trained with the coverage-targeted losses
of \S\ref{sec:method} never win on any dataset. Given that these losses
were the intended methodological contribution, we state this plainly: on
tabular selective prediction at this scale, the added machinery does not
pay for itself.

\textbf{Coverage-targeted losses require a quantile threshold to be
stable.} Training the operating threshold $\tau$ as a free parameter
alongside the network is unstable: on Electricity it produced AURC
$0.3455$ with standard deviation $0.0951$ and a worst seed of $0.4606$ --
worse than random abstention. Deriving $\tau$ instead as the empirical
$\kappa$-quantile of the batch's scores makes the target coverage hold by
construction and reduces the seed standard deviation roughly eightfold
(to $0.0118$), with the worst seed improving to $0.2681$.

\textbf{What predicts whether aggregation helps? We do not know.} Two
candidate explanations are contradicted by our own data. Class count fails
because German Credit ($K{=}2$) benefits while Adult and Electricity
($K{=}2$) do not, and because Satimage benefits despite its entropy signal
remaining rank-identical to MSP. The quality of the geometric (Tier-C)
signals also fails: Electricity and German Credit have an identical
best-Tier-C-to-MSP AURC ratio of $1.16$ and opposite outcomes, and Letter
benefits despite a weak ratio of $1.60$. We report the selection question
as open rather than advance a mechanism our results do not support.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Scope.} Six tabular datasets with gradient-boosted trees. We
make no claim about neural base models, image data, or larger scales; the
degeneracy proof is architecture-independent, but every empirical result
here is tabular. Only one dataset carries a natural distribution shift
(Electricity, and its label drift is weak), so our evidence on RQ2 is thin
-- which is precisely why we do not claim the regime-dependence we
pre-registered.

\textbf{Effect sizes are small.} The benefit where it exists is
$6.2$--$6.4\%$ relative AURC. It is consistent and statistically
supported, but a practitioner should weigh it against the cost of
maintaining a signal bank, a meta-split and a second trained model.

\textbf{The selection question is unresolved.} We cannot predict which
datasets benefit, which limits the result's prescriptive value: we can say
aggregation sometimes helps and can quantify by how much, but not tell a
practitioner in advance whether their dataset is one of the fortunate
ones.

\textbf{Statistical scope.} Ten seeds per configuration bounds the
per-seed Wilcoxon test: with Holm correction across 22 methods, only a
unanimous 10/10 result can reach $p<0.05$, so we rely on the paired
bootstrap for finer effects and report both.

\textbf{Operational costs.} The conformal guarantee assumes
exchangeability and is therefore valid in-distribution only; Tier-C
signals require retaining the training set at inference time; and the
meta-split needed to train the aggregator is itself a real cost on small
datasets such as German Credit, whose 150-row test split also makes its
estimates the noisiest we report.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We set out to test whether aggregating heterogeneous uncertainty signals
improves selective prediction over the best single signal. The most
durable result is a negative one with a proof: on binary tasks the
logit-derived signal family is rank-degenerate, so a bank of six
confidence measures supplies exactly one ordering and no aggregator over
it can beat MSP. This is a structural explanation for a pattern of
negative results in the literature, and it implies a concrete practical
rule -- when assembling a signal bank, verify that the signals induce
\emph{different rankings}, because plausible-sounding diversity is not
sufficient.

Where the bank is non-degenerate, aggregation helps by a consistent but
modest $6.2$--$6.4\%$ on three of six datasets, and harms on two. We could
not identify what distinguishes the two groups; neither class count nor
geometric-signal quality predicts it, and we report this as open rather
than fit a mechanism after the fact. Three secondary findings are
practically relevant and negative: ensemble disagreement never helped, the
simplest aggregator beat the neural ones we designed, and abstention
widened rather than narrowed subgroup disparities. Finally, we note that
the choice of risk-control bound is not a detail: switching from Hoeffding
to Hoeffding--Bentkus moves distribution-free selective-risk guarantees
from vacuous to usable at practical risk levels.
